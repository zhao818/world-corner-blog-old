Trust series · upstream (the supply side). The origin essay covered the demand side — how the individual machine lives on “predictability”; Precondition Theory covered how the individual walks through this era; Group-Side Observation covered where the era is heading. All three kept using the word “predictability,” yet none answered the more upstream question: who, exactly, is producing predictability, in an unbroken stream? This essay supplies that upstream. Positioning: what it completes is the layer never opened in Origin §1.3 — “morality is a part, not the chassis; that layer is dismantled in the next section” — the machine that manufactures “keeping one’s word.” With it in place, the half-sentence left hanging in the origin essay finally lands. Reading order: not mandatory. To build the coordinate system first, start with the origin essay (the demand side’s foundation; this is its supply side). To go straight to “where predictability comes from,” start here. The two interlock: one machine, one assembly line. Four writing rules, same as the origin essay: ① this is a model, a tool for understanding, not an object of faith; ② every universal claim carries its own limits — “I have repeatedly seen this shape,” never “it has always been so”; ③ this essay takes no side — it does not say which ethics is correct, only how ethics arises, how it breaks, who produces it, and how to repair it; ④ it uses no private engineering cases, only everyday experience common to all humanity as anchor points.

0. Opening: One IOU Divides Two Worlds#

There is an action almost everyone has performed, yet almost no one has paused to consider — writing an IOU.

An acquaintance wants to borrow money. If the amount is at all large, you ask them to write one. As they write, the sentence in your head is: I’m afraid it won’t be clear later; better to keep a record.

But the same borrowing, from your parents — you would never ask for an IOU. Your closest friend borrows two hundred yuan in a pinch — no IOU either.

The same action — handing money over. Where is the difference?

The difference is in the IOU that was never written.

You lend to your parents and your closest friend not because they signed anything, but because you trust that “they will repay even without a record.” That backing — repayment even without paper — is what this essay is about. It has a name: ethics.

Don’t rush to file this under moral instruction. Look at how sharp the line drawn by that IOU is: with one stroke of the pen, the same lending shifts in meaning from “I trust you’ll repay” to “I guard against your not repaying.” An IOU is not evidence that affection has thinned. It is the boundary where ethics exits and law takes over.

Why does this line exist? Because with parents and closest friends you have accumulated decades of “keeping your word” — you can plan without documentation. With a half-familiar person you do not yet have that predictability, so a sheet of paper must stand guard in ethics’ place. It is not that strangers are worse. It is that between strangers, that backing has not yet grown.

At this point the essay gives a functional definition, not a doctrinal one:

Ethics is the mutual backing between people that requires no signature or seal.

It is the entire set of defaults behind “I’ll lend you my money because I trust you’ll repay”: promises must count; lying should make you blush; taking advantage should make you uneasy; lending a hand to someone in trouble is not a loss but a duty. Each of these defaults, taken alone, looks like “virtue.” Together they perform one engineering job: making one person’s behavior computable in advance by another.

And “computing others accurately” is exactly the nail the origin essay drove in: trust is a gregarious species’ dependence on the predictability of others’ behavior. Trust is your machine for receiving predictability. What this essay flips around to examine is the transmitting side — who, and by what means, sends out these behavior-computable signals, year after year.

Phenomena first, naming second. Now, starting from “that invisible IOU,” we walk backward step by step: where it comes from, what makes it believable, and who repairs it when it breaks.

I. Boundaries Are Ground Out, Not Handed Down by Sages#

1.1 Start by Reasoning From “No Ethics”#

To see what ethics is, the dumbest and most reliable method is to first imagine a world without it.

Imagine two people cooperating for the first time. The first thinks: if I work hard and he slacks, what then? If he slackens first, don’t I lose? The second thinks the same. So both wait for the other to move first, both afraid of baring their earnestness only to have it stepped on. The result: nothing gets done — not because they are bad, but because they cannot compute each other.

This is the eternal problem of gregarious life: to live well, the individual instinctively tends to self first; yet once everyone is self-first, cooperation dissolves, and gregarious creatures cannot survive without cooperation. Individual and group are in tension here.

How to untangle it? Did a sage descend to set the rules? — History indeed has sages, prophets, and lawgivers who left written commandments. But what this essay wants to show is a plainer shape: before any sage appeared, boundaries had already been ground out by ordinary people, through countless rounds of friction.

1.2 The Grinding: Countless Rounds of “Crossing — Losing — Correcting”#

Two people cooperating repeatedly go through round after round of trial and error:

You took more this time; I give you less next time. Your word didn’t hold this time; I don’t trust you next time. Conversely: you kept your word three times, so I dare entrust you with something bigger; you extended a hand when I was in trouble, so I remember to repay.

No judge, no statute. Punishment is “I won’t trust you next time”; reward is “I’ll trust you more next time.” Round after round of this primitive reward-and-punishment, and the group gradually deposits a set of measures everyone defaults to: where my desire stops and your territory begins; what may be said and what may not be done; advantage taken must be compensated; favors owed must be repaid.

Nobody invented these measures. They were survived into existence — groups that never ground out these measures tore themselves apart with internal friction and disbanded early; only the ones that ground them out passed them down. So the shape this essay keeps seeing is: any human group that survives long, on any continent, under any faith, grows roughly the same set of boundaries — don’t steal, don’t deceive, don’t kill, keep your word, pull the stranger out of the well. The commandments’ wording varies endlessly; the measures beneath are startlingly uniform.

The uniformity itself is the point: it doesn’t look like one sage’s exclusive invention. It looks like the factory requirement of gregariousness itself — without these boundaries, group living doesn’t run.

Boundaries were not decreed by sages. They are the shape that gregarious creatures ground themselves into, through countless rounds of crossing, losing, and correcting.

(A self-limit: this doesn’t mean sages and lawgivers don’t matter — their work was to fix already-ground measures into text and spread them with authority. The essay only corrects the sequence: first the grinding, then the decree. The grinding is original; the decree is transcription.)

1.3 Law Is Ethics’ IOU#

With “ground-out boundaries” as the ground, the rules between people sort into two kinds of goods:

One kind is unwritten — the consideration between family members, promise-keeping between friends, the basic tacit agreement among strangers of “don’t cut the line; keep your word.” It is everywhere, yet not one line of it is in any legal code. This essay calls this good ethics.

The other kind is welded shut — robbery gets sentenced, debts get repaid, contracts get performed; black on white, with police and courts, enforced if breached. This good is law.

Now for the essay’s first knife. We usually describe the relationship as “law is the institutionalization of ethics” — correct, but too flat. A sharper phrasing:

Law is ethics’ IOU — only the accounts ethics cannot collect get handed to law.

What does this mean? Look first at where ethics collects well: family borrows without IOUs — ethics already collected, the account settled inside “affection” and “long-term mutual trust,” no need for law to lift a finger. The moment ethics can’t collect — the other side repudiates the debt; or these are two strangers — that “receivable credit” goes bad and gets handed to law: IOUs, courts, enforcement.

So behind every article of law stands a past in which ethics failed first. Behind contract law: countless “verbal promise, then denial.” Behind criminal law: countless “the strong preying on the weak with no one to intervene.” Law is humanity’s patch after being wounded by its own ethics — using force to collect what ethics could not.

This is not to rank law beneath ethics. Quite the opposite — law has two things ethics lacks: force (ethics relies on conscience, and conscience collapses) and uniformity (ethics is a ruler in each person’s chest; law is one ruler for the whole city). Human society’s rules have always been these two layers stacked: ethics handles the broad, daily under-layer of backing; law handles the top layer, the fatal guarantee. Ethics governs “most of the time”; law governs “the final moment.”

With these two layers clear, section two can answer this essay’s most pressing question.

II. Ethics Is Trust’s Water Line#

2.1 Claiming the Sentence the Origin Essay Left Hanging#

Before continuing, an awkward but unavoidable task: to claim, myself, a sentence left hanging in the origin essay.

Origin §1.3 said — morality is the lubricant maintaining predictability, a part on the apparatus, not the chassis. It even left a thread: “The chassis is another layer. That layer is dismantled in the next section.” — and that section never opened. It did name the suppliers once (old sources replaced by contracts and verification), then stopped, never laying out the whole production line. This essay completes that unfinished move.

That sentence was fine within the trust essay, but lifted out, it reads as a dangerous conclusion: if morality is merely a part, is “keeping one’s word” dispensable?

Filling exactly that misreading-gap is this essay’s job, and it requires a lens change.

The origin essay stands in front of a single machine — watching how the individual “trust machine” receives predictability, starves, breaks. In that lens, honesty, promise-keeping, and kindness are indeed parts on the machine.

This essay steps back and stands in front of the whole water line. From here you see another scene: honesty, promise-keeping, kindness did not fall from the sky as parts to install. They are manufactured one by one by a social mechanism and then installed into each person’s heart. That a person keeps their word is not innate — it is steeped in: watching adults keep promises as a child, being rescued by someone’s kept word, watching promise-breakers punished in front of you, round after round. This production mechanism that makes keeping promises profitable and breaking them expensive — that is ethics.

So for the things the origin essay called “parts,” this essay finds the workshop:

Ethics is not a part of morality. It is the workshop that manufactures moral parts. The origin essay covered the demand side — how the individual depends on predictability. This essay covers the supply side — how society manufactures predictability in an unbroken stream. One machine, one assembly line, two essays; no conflict.

Parts and workshop are not two things; they are two faces of one set of norms — parts when viewed from a single machine, workshop when viewed from the whole network.

This also strings the essay’s three descriptions into one line: the opening called ethics “mutual backing” — the water line’s delivered goods; section one called it “ground-out measures” — the production spec; this section calls it the workshop — production itself. Three descriptions, one thing.

The origin essay’s phrase “morality supplies predictability” — this essay simply unfolds the word “supplies” into the whole assembly line.

2.2 Translating Ethics Into Production Processes, Item by Item#

So that “ethics manufactures predictability” isn’t just a pretty phrase, translate them into processes one by one. The things we call “virtues,” viewed on the assembly line, each have a definite job:

Sincerity makes signals readable. Someone who speaks straight, without hidden layers, saves you the cost of decoding how many layers each sentence has. Conversely, when everyone speaks in riddles, every sentence becomes a reading-comprehension exercise, and predictability goes out of stock — you cannot compute what they actually mean.

Promise-keeping makes commitments redeemable. He says the fifth, it’s the fifth; you say fine, it’s fine. This one is the most direct: it turns “the future” into something plannable — I can promise you something for the day after tomorrow because I know you’ll do as you said.

Responsibility makes consequences receivable. When something breaks: no blame-shifting, no playing blind. This one governs what wounds predictability worst — “the mess nobody picks up.” If every mess leaves everyone guessing who’ll take it, cooperation hangs in midair forever.

Fairness makes exchange sustainable. Fifty-fifty means fifty-fifty; no swapping the weights. This one governs the long run: cheat me once and I may bear it; cheat me every time and I stop coming. Fairness is the lubricant of “cooperation that can keep being cooperation.”

Compassion keeps the floor from giving way. When others fall into trouble, a hand extended is duty. This one looks the “softest” but governs the hardest scene: when a person is at the end of their rope, can they still count on the group? In a group you can count on, people dare hand themselves over; in one they can’t, everyone self-preserves first, and predictability snaps precisely at the moment of crisis.

See — in a moral-education class these are five exhortations to virtue. On the assembly line they are five processes guaranteeing “computable behavior.” They are not five separate virtues; they are five valves on one pumping station — lose any one, and you haven’t lost one virtue; you’ve lost one supply line.

Ethical norms are, in essence, the production processes supplying “behavioral predictability.” “Good ethics” does not mean a place’s people are kinder; it means the pumping station here is more stable — what you hand out will most likely be caught.

This is the same sentence as the origin essay’s “a trust-abundant society isn’t morally better; its behavioral system is more predictable” — with the lens switched from the receiving end to the transmitting end.

2.3 A Boundary: Ethics Is the Trunk Line, Not the Only Pipe#

Stop here, or this essay will commit the error of origin rule ② — declaring ethics the only source of predictability.

Ethics is the trunk line, not the only pipe. Predictability has other suppliers, listed here so the reader doesn’t take a universal claim from me:

Law, supplying by force — breach gets adjudicated, so you dare sign with strangers; Institutions, supplying by process — the company has procedures, so a colleague quitting mid-task doesn’t sink you; the process picks it up; Institutions and third-party verification, supplying by endorsement — you don’t know the doctor, but you trust the hospital; you don’t know the landlord, but you trust the agency.

And a fourth channel, the widest-laid in recent years: rating and reputation systems, supplying by collective experience — you dare order from an online shop not because you know the seller, but because tens of thousands of strangers gave him five stars first. This channel is the most special: it is neither ground out nor poured in. It is the grinding, digitized — millions of people, across screens, grinding “is this shop trustworthy” in real time; the platform merely moved the grinding stone from the neighborhood onto the internet.

Where do these channels differ from ethics? In supply cost.

Ethics is the cheapest channel: no police, no contracts, no vetting — it runs on the steeped-in, universally-defaulted tacit layer between family, old friends, and neighbors. The overwhelming majority of mutual trust travels this channel, at zero cost. Law, institutions, and verification each require maintaining enormous machinery — far more expensive.

So humanity’s optimal has never been “replace ethics with law,” nor “rely on ethics alone and build no institutions.” It is: use ethics, the cheap channel, as broadly as possible for the base layer, and deploy the expensive machines — law, institutions — only to guarantee the most fatal edges. The day a society can no longer supply even the cheap channel of ethics, every expensive machine overloads at once — because demand flooding toward courts, contracts, and verification exceeds what the machines can process. That is the weight of ethics as the trunk line: when it is cut, the other pipes still exist — they’re just all insufficient.

III. The Scale of Civilization, and the One Thing Animals Lack#

3.1 Can Ethics’ Refinement Measure a Civilization?#

Before writing this section, a word of history: using an era’s ethical refinement to measure its civilization is an old idea — from Confucius “governing with virtue” to the modern compliment “people here follow the rules,” it is the same ruler.

But this essay won’t enshrine that idea as an iron law. It only wants to give it a landable anchor, so it can be observed rather than felt.

The shape this essay keeps seeing is: the most honest gauge of a society’s ethical refinement is not how it treats acquaintances. It is how it treats strangers.

Keeping faith with acquaintances — nearly every society manages that; it’s ground out by long-term reciprocity, nothing rare. What actually separates water levels is the layer of promise-keeping between strangers with no dealings and no ongoing relationship:

  • When you queue, will the person behind cut in? (Keeping faith with “unseen competitors”)
  • The taxi driver taking you across town — does he take the long way? (Keeping faith with the “one-time customer”)
  • A stranger online; you pledge a donation. Does it arrive? (Keeping faith with “people you will never see again”)
  • Toward the desperate, the weak, those with nothing to offer in return — does the society extend a hand or walk around? (Keeping faith with “people with no exchange value at all”)

These four questions don’t ask “are there many good people.” They ask: does this water system reach the people who have no accounts with you? A society’s water system supplies family, then friends, then colleagues, then total strangers — wherever it reaches, that is the water level of its civilization. To gauge an era’s civilization, rather than measuring how high it built or how sharp its weapons, watch whether it dares turn its back to a stranger.

(A self-limit: this is one scale among several, not the only gauge. A society also has readings for rule of law, technical capacity, institutional resilience — ethics is one ruler. This essay claims only that this ruler reads something true, not that it measures everything.)

3.2 First, Correct an Error That Invites a One-Line Rebuttal#

Now for a surgery. The original draft (or rather, a version many people love to tell) contained this claim: animals have no good and evil, no empathy, no responsibility; humans are the only beings that broke through instinct; ethics is the essential difference between humans and animals.

It sounds rousing. It doesn’t stand — and it’s the kind of not-standing that gets toppled by one line from anyone who knows the field.

Decades of observations by the primatologist Frans de Waal are on the table: capuchin monkeys faced with “same work, different pay” refuse their cucumber share; chimpanzees actively reconcile after violent conflict, grooming each other; elephants rescue distressed companions — in documented cases, some of the rescued were not kin. Empathy, reciprocity, a sense of fairness — the prototype of morality exists widely in the animal world. It is not humanity’s exclusive invention.

So “animals know no good and evil; only humans have ethics” cannot be printed as-is. Print it, and the first comment from a knowledgeable reader is “go read de Waal” — and that one line is enough to deflate the whole essay’s credibility.

3.3 So What Is Special About Humans: Not Doing Good — Quarreling Over Good#

But your intuition holds something true — on morality, humans and animals are indeed two kinds of beings. Only the dividing line sits somewhere else.

Animals have empathy, reciprocity, fairness-sense — but they do not quarrel over these things. A chimpanzee does not stop to ask: “Is this reconciliation actually fair? Should fairness follow effort or need?” The capuchin refuses the cucumber from an instinctive sense of unfairness, but it will not open a debate on “distributive justice,” let alone write the two camps’ positions into scriptures and transmit them for millennia.

Humans do. Humans are the only species that convenes a standing assembly on “should” and “ought” — and that assembly has been in session for thousands of years without adjourning:

  • “Sacrifice the few to save the many — right or wrong?” Some say yes; some say never.
  • “Does the well-meant lie count as a lie?” Some say no; some say yes.
  • “Does one keep faith with enemies too?” Some call it the bottom line; others call it cruelty to one’s own.

No other animal splits into camps over “what does kindness mean” and quarrels for millennia, even goes to war over it. The prototype of morality, animals have. But the questioning, correcting, rebelling against, and remaking of morality itself — that is exclusively human. Other creatures live by instinctive reciprocity; humans refuse to stop at reciprocity — they must interrogate “what reciprocity is right,” then rewrite their own rules of behavior according to their answers, over and over.

So the meaning of “ethics defines the human” survives, with the blade re seated:

Humans are not the animals that do good. They are the animals that haggle with “good.” What defines the human is not ethics itself, but humanity’s never-ending interrogation of ethics.

This phrasing has an extra benefit — it no longer demeans animals, and it states ethics’ origin more precisely: precisely because the animal side already holds the raw material of empathy and reciprocity, ethics is not a set of commandments dropped from heaven. It is the animal’s existing reciprocal instinct, amplified, abstracted, and turned into principles that can be argued. An amplification, not an invention. So this essay dislikes the phrase “humans broke through instinct,” preferring: humanity took the small ember of reciprocity in instinct and fanned it into a fire large enough to illuminate and to conflagrate — it can light up civilization, and it can burn civilization down. Which is exactly the next section: when the fire grows, someone wants to own it.

IV. Ethics Has Factories, and Factories Have Owners#

4.1 Ethics Is Not Only Ground Out — It Is Also Manufactured#

The foregoing — ethics is ground out — is correct but incomplete. Grinding is one source. There is another, and it has been gaining ground in the modern era: ethics is not only grown. It is also made, deliberately, by organization.

Think how you learned “what is right” — not entirely ground out in living; a large share was taught into you:

  • In primary school you recited “helping others is a virtue; lying is shameful”;
  • At the company annual meeting, leadership taught you “wolf culture is drive; slacking is shameful”;
  • On trending topics, cycle after cycle of “downfall — pile-on — social death” taught you “this is wrong, and doing it costs”;
  • In prison, inmates are re-educated to “reform and start anew”;
  • In advertising, brands repeat: “buying this is how you love your family.”

These are not measures you ground out in dealing with people. They are designed and poured into you. The same behavior under a different value system gets a different label overnight: the same “don’t take losses” is taught as “shrewdness” in one place and “slyness” in another; the same “obedience” is “loyalty” in one organization and “servility” in another. The behavior is the behavior. The label is set by whoever pours.

So the essay’s second knife:

Ethics comes in two kinds: the ground-out kind and the manufactured kind. Any institution that can mass-shape a population’s sense of “what counts as right” — schools, companies, media, platforms, courts, prisons — is an ethics factory.

4.2 One Layer Deeper: Factories Have Owners#

This observation alone isn’t new — everyone knows schools teach conduct. The original draft stopped at the “positive energy” surface. This essay digs one layer deeper, because beneath this layer sits a question nobody is comfortable with, and nobody gets around:

If ethics is manufactured, there are factories; where there are factories, someone runs the factories — someone decides which “right” the assembly line produces.

Schools can teach self-sacrifice or teach obedience-supreme, depending on who writes the textbooks. Media can amplify one voice and mute another, depending on who holds the editor’s chair. Prisons can rehabilitate or destroy, depending on which theory of rehabilitation they execute. A platform’s trending list can crown one value system and, overnight, brand another “politically incorrect” — depending on whose hand stands behind the algorithm.

Whoever controls these factories is installing the factory defaults of good and evil into an entire generation — writing “what counts as right” into people’s base-layer settings before they can question anything. This is mightier than ordering people around: commands govern a moment; defaults govern a lifetime — and the poured-in mistake it for their own thinking.

Group-Side Observation asked, “Who supervises the rebuilder?” — that question aimed at power holders. This essay supplies the other half:

Who supervises the factory defaults of right and wrong? Power holders fight over power; ethics-factory owners fight over definition — and often, these are the same people doing both.

What a power holder wants most has never been the army. It is the definitional power that “my words are morality, and those who oppose me are degenerate.” With it, he need not persuade you one by one — he only needs society’s factories to manufacture “obedience to him” as “goodness.”

4.3 Nail the Boundaries Immediately, Before the Knife Cuts Its Own#

Having said “factories,” we must stop at once and nail the boundaries, or this essay slides into a conspiracy theory in which every school and institution deceives and all “manufactured ethics” is bad. Not so. Four points:

First: manufactured ethics is not automatically bad ethics. Public education teaching honesty, courts deterring breach, media promoting helping others — in many decent societies, the top-poured ethics points in the same direction as the folk-ground ethics. The school’s “don’t lie” and the street’s “don’t deceive” are the same thing. Here the factory is the pumping station’s amplifier, not its polluter.

Second: ground-out ethics is not automatically good either. The street-ground “don’t take advantage” is good. But some ground-out rules — “women shouldn’t be seen in public,” “outsiders are all thieves” — are bad rules, and precisely because they come “handed down from the ancestors,” they are extra-hard to change. Natively-ground ethics loses in being unwritten and brittle, and it loses in mistaking prejudice for tradition.

Third: the true watershed is not “ground” versus “made” — it is whether challenge is permitted. Ground-out ethics gets re-ground by new friction — this generation finds it wrong, the next grinds out a new one. Manufactured ethics, if it too can be discussed, overturned, remade, differs not at all in essence from the ground-out kind. What is truly dangerous is not “someone shaping ethics.” It is the shaper denying that he shapes — pouring, while proclaiming “this is heaven’s law, always has been, beyond question.” Whenever a set of ethics declares itself “beyond discussion, beyond change,” ask one more question: who taught you this sentence, and what are they after?

Fourth: nail the fourth point into a ruler — an ethics’ health is measured not by how correct it is, but by whether it leaves itself an opening for being overturned. Ethics that permits challenge can be corrected when wrong. Ethics that forbids challenge rots even when right — because it stays forever in the era that invented it, unable to follow new human circumstances.

Finally, one rule carried over from the origin essay, so no reader uses this essay for point-scoring: it names no specific factory, institution, or era — it describes mechanisms, not labels. Whoever puts the “factory owner” hat onto a specific real-world unit misuses this essay. This is an observation lens, not a verdict.

4.4 Back to the Water: What Ethics Fears Is Not Supply Cuts — It Is a Single Source#

Assemble the four sections and return to the main line: ethics supplies trust’s water.

Ground-out ethics is countless small neighborhood water plants supplying: each house has its own measures; if one household’s rules grow badly, the neighbor still has other water. This “distributed supply” looks messy, but it survives cuts — one pipe cracks, the others still flow.

Manufactured ethics is the metropolitan water plant: efficient, wide coverage, capable of switching an entire nation onto one “right and wrong” overnight. But this centralized supply has one fatal weakness — once the plant’s owner takes the source, or the plant produces a defective batch, everyone drinks the contamination, and with only one producer, even the option of “switching suppliers” doesn’t exist.

So this essay’s attitude toward “ethics factories” is not anti-factory. It is anti-monopoly:

What ethics’ supply fears most is not factories everywhere. It is this-and-only-this. The ground, the taught, the argued-and-corrected — many channels supply; one channel breaks, the others hold. A single-source right-and-wrong, once wrong, is a whole society drinking dirty water with nowhere to switch.

This is also why “quarreling over good” is so precious — the quarreling is those small folk water plants continuously producing new sources. A society that lets ethics be publicly argued keeps all its small plants open. A society that strangles argument and permits one voice welds every pipe into one. The former is messy, but it survives. The latter is tidy — and when the one pipe breaks, everything breaks.

Epilogue: Turning the Lamp on Myself#

Written this far, by the old custom, the final step is turning the lamp on myself.

This essay claimed ethics is trust’s water line, that boundaries are ground out, that ethics has factories, that owners fight over definition. So — who is saying these words?

Someone living through a water outage.

My generation happens to live in an era of “predictability supply cuts” — today a guru collapses, tomorrow a conscientious company blows up, the day after a single post leaves someone with no way to defend themselves. Precisely because I stand at the scene of the outage, I turned to look at how the pipes were laid. In an era with ample security, ethics would be like air — no one would write a treatise “On Air,” because it is too natural, natural to the point of needing no explanation. So this essay is not transcendent truth. It is a maintenance report written from inside a water outage. It is useful because it stands at the same outage as its readers. The day the water comes back, it will probably look superfluous — which would be a good thing.

And a falsification opening must be left, so this essay does not become the “beyond-discussion ethics” it criticizes.

The essay’s entire claim compresses to one sentence: ethics is society’s trunk production line supplying “behavioral predictability”; it is ground out of group friction and also manufactured in batches by factories; its mark of health is permitting many supply channels — and permitting itself to be overturned.

This sentence holds under conditions. If a society could be found — ethics widely dissolved, everyone harboring schemes — where predictability nonetheless stayed abundant for the long term, with all mutual trust carried by pure law, pure institutions, pure technology, then this essay’s framework fails; the water line is not a necessity. The shape I keep seeing so far: not found. Wherever ethics suffers mass supply cuts, law and institutions overload at the same time.

Someone will surely raise ratings, contracts, blockchain — technology can already replace ethics. This essay’s reply: the water technology supplies governs narrow-domain transactions — payments, fulfillment, ordering, inspection; bounded exchanges that contracts and ratings genuinely handle. But the bulk of human trust was never in the narrow domain: marriage and funerals, raising children, entrusting a life-threatening illness, handing your house keys to a stranger at midnight — these general domains have no black on white and no rating dimensions; smart contracts cannot encode them. Technology reaches the counter. Ethics’ cheap water supplies the vast ground technology cannot reach.

But I do not treat “not found yet” as “nonexistent.” If you find such a society — you are welcome to overturn me.

Finally, nailing down the relationships among the essays, closing the circuit of the series:

The origin essay asked: what does the individual trust machine live on? Answer: predictability. This essay asked: who produces predictability? Answer: ethics, the water line. Precondition Theory and Group-Side Observation asked: when the water is cut, how does the individual walk, and where does the era go?

Four essays, one thread — supply, demand, individual, group. Strung on one thread they form a complete map; pulled apart, each is only a corner of it.

This essay ends here. The rest is left to the people living through the outage to verify: of the “keeping one’s word” around you, how much rides on ground-out tacit understanding, and how much on poured-in rules? And that sentence you believe most firmly — “this is right” — who put it into your head, and when?
